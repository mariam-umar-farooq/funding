Borrowing and Sharing Ownership

⏱ About 14 minutes

The remaining funding families do carry a cost — either repayment with interest (debt) or a share of the enterprise (equity). Used well, they unlock growth that own-resources and grants can't reach.

Lending / debt instruments

Provide funding in exchange for repayment of the principal plus interest, on pre-agreed terms, sometimes requiring guarantees. Variants include concessional and flexible loans (low or no interest, extended terms) and crowd/peer-to-peer lending — matching lenders and borrowers via an online platform, with lower overheads than banks.

✓ Pros

Good for financing assets and equipment that boost efficiency or production; the revenue gains can service the debt.

✕ Cons

Set repayment terms must be met regardless of performance — a struggling business still owes the instalments.
Equity

Unlike debt, equity capital is received in exchange for a share of the company (typically stock). It gives a critical capital base to grow operations, access other finance, and reduce risk for other investors. A financing member contributes capital to help establish the company and carry out its social activity. Venture capital is equity administered through VC firms.

✓ Pros (venture capital)

Large injections of capital to scale quickly; usually invested once the model and market fit are proven.

✕ Cons

You give up a share of ownership — and often some control — of the mission-driven enterprise.
⚖️ Debt keeps your ownership but demands fixed repayments. Equity shares ownership but flexes with performance. Which fits a social enterprise better, and when?

Social Impact Bonds & Mixed Tools

⏱ About 12 minutes

Beyond the classic families, social finance has produced some innovative, hybrid instruments — designed to tie money directly to social outcomes.

Social impact bond (SIB). A contract with the public sector: it pays for better social outcomes in a given area and passes part of the savings achieved on to investors. Despite the name, it's not a bond — repayment and return depend entirely on achieving the desired social outcomes, which makes it a relatively risky investment.
🔀
Mezzanine finance — neither pure equity nor pure debt. Earns a higher return than pure debt but less than equity, and carries higher risk than debt but less than equity. Often used in later-stage growth.
🌐
Crowd / peer-to-peer lending — online platforms matching many small lenders to borrowers, often at better terms than traditional institutions for both sides.
🧭 The six families, in one view: (1) self-financing, (2) friends & family, (3) grants, (4) management of surpluses & assets, (5) lending/debt, (6) equity — with social impact bonds and mezzanine as hybrids that blend features. Most real social enterprises use a mix, matched to their stage.
🎯 A social impact bond only pays investors if real outcomes are achieved. What's attractive — and what's risky — about tying money to outcomes that way?
