Who Supplies the Money

⏱ About 12 minutes

Before choosing a funding tool, it helps to know who the money can come from. Social enterprises draw on a wider range of suppliers than ordinary businesses — because people fund them not only for a return, but for the mission.

Main financial suppliers for the social economy
👤
Private citizens — capital from individuals, mostly as donations or small investments. Online crowdfunding platforms have made this source far easier to tap.
🤝
Members, shareholders & other stakeholders — capital supplied by the members and community of the enterprise itself.
🏦
Financial intermediaries — ethical and social banks, solidarity finance, and banks with philanthropic projects or product lines for social-economy organisations.
🏛️
Public sector — subsidies and incentives to social enterprises and to those who fund them.
🛡️ A special supporter — guarantee funds. The EU's EaSI (Employment and Social Innovation) Guarantee Instrument is dedicated to microfinance and social entrepreneurship. It doesn't lend directly — it guarantees loans, so banks and lenders are more willing to finance social enterprises in their start-up and development phases.

Social Investment, Investor & Finance

⏱ About 14 minutes

A distinctive feature of social-enterprise funding is that some of the money comes with a social mission attached. Three related terms describe this world — they overlap, but each means something specific.

Three terms, defined
Social investment
Funding that pursues an accountable social, cultural or environmental purpose; is independent of the state; puts the mission of the investee first; is transparent about measuring and reporting its social impact; and is structured to create financial value or community capacity over time.
The social investor
Invests primarily to support a vision of a better world. They may seek market-comparable returns where those still benefit the investee — but they'll often make concessions in favour of the mission and the impact.
Social finance
Funding to achieve a social as well as a financial return. Same characteristics as social investment, except the funding need not be repayable — grants and gifts matter as much to social enterprises as equity does to private companies.
💡 The thread running through all three: the social mission is not a side effect of the money — it's the point of it. That's what sets this apart from conventional investment, where financial return is the sole aim.
